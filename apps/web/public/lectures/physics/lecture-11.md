### 1. The Vocabulary of Image Formation

**Core Idea**
A precise set of terms describes every mirror (and lens) problem — distances measured along the principal axis, heights perpendicular to it.

**Explanation**
- **Principal axis:** the normal line to the midpoint of the surface — that midpoint is the **vertex V** (or **pole P**).
- **Object location (O) / image location (I):** the positions of object and image on the principal axis; **object distance p** and **image distance q** are measured from the object/image to the mirror.
- **Object size (h) / image size (h′):** the heights; h′ is **positive for an upright image** (same direction as the object) and **negative for an inverted image** (opposite direction).
- **Magnification:** **M = h′/h**, the ratio of image size to object size.
- **Focal point (F, "Focus"):** the point where **parallel rays** reflect (mirrors) or refract (lenses) so as to pass through — or appear to originate from; **focal length (f):** the distance from F to the mirror.
- **Center of curvature (C) / radius of curvature (R):** the center and radius of the sphere from which the surface is cut; R is the distance between C and the surface.
- **For mirrors: R = 2f.**
- For far-away sources (infinity), the incoming rays are effectively **parallel** (plane waves) — that is exactly the condition defining the focal point.

**Example**
A mirror with R = 20 cm has f = 10 cm: F sits halfway between the vertex and C. A distant object (the Sun) sends parallel rays that converge at F.

**Key Point**
R = 2f; distant objects → parallel rays → image at F. All distances are referenced to the vertex along the principal axis.

### 2. Real vs. Virtual Images

**Core Idea**
A real image forms where light rays **actually converge**; a virtual image forms where their **backward extensions** meet.

**Explanation**

| | **Real image** | **Virtual image** |
|---|---|---|
| Formed from | Intersection of the **reflected rays** (mirrors) / refracted rays (lenses) | Intersection of the **extensions** of reflected/refracted rays |
| Screen? | **Can** be viewed on a screen | **Cannot** be viewed on a screen |
| Location | In **front of** mirrors, behind (back of) lenses | **Behind** (back of) mirrors, in front of lenses |
| Sign of q | **+** | **−** |

The screen test is the physical discriminator: if light actually lands there, you can catch it; if your eye merely traces diverging rays backward, you cannot.

**Example**
Light reflected by a curved makeup mirror that converges in front of it → real, projectable. The "you" behind a bathroom mirror → virtual, visible but uncatchable.

**Key Point**
Real = converging rays = screen-catchable = q > 0. Virtual = traced-back rays = not projectable = q < 0 — but perfectly *visible*.

### 3. Plane Mirrors

**Core Idea**
A flat mirror always forms a **virtual, upright, unmagnified** image exactly as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

**Explanation**
Take a point source at O. Rays leave, reflect, and **diverge** — but an observer traces them back to a point I behind the mirror: the image. Two rays do the construction:
1. A ray from P traveling horizontally (PQ) hits the mirror perpendicular to itself and **reflects straight back**.
2. A second ray (PR) reflects obeying the law of reflection.

An observer traces both reflected rays backward to their apparent origin P′. The triangles **PQR and P′QR are identical**, so PQ = P′Q — the image is exactly as deep as the object:

**|p| = |q|, i.e., p = −q** and **M = −q/p = h′/h = +1** (for any flat mirror).

Full image properties: (1) as far behind as the object is in front; (2) unmagnified (M = +1, |h′| = |h|), virtual (q is −ve), and upright (M and h′ are +ve). For real objects, flat-mirror images are **always** virtual.

One signature quirk: a flat mirror produces an **apparent left–right reversal** — stand in front of one and raise your right hand, and the image raises the hand on the opposite side. (The source states the phenomenon; a deeper account of *why* is [SOURCE DOES NOT SPECIFY].)

**Example**
You stand 2.0 m from a flat mirror: your image is 2.0 m behind it (q = −2.0 m), M = +1, upright, virtual. The image is 4.0 m from your eyes.

**Key Point**
Plane mirror: q = −p, M = +1, virtual, upright — proven by two rays and identical triangles.

### 4. Spherical Mirrors: Converging and Diverging

**Core Idea**
A **concave** mirror is a **converging** mirror (f > 0, with F and C in front — real focus); a **convex** mirror is a **diverging** mirror (f < 0, with F and C behind — virtual focus).

**Explanation**
Both are cut from a sphere of radius R = 2f. For the concave mirror, parallel incoming rays reflect and actually pass through F **in front** of the mirror, and C sits in front too. For the convex mirror, reflected rays diverge, but their extensions meet at F **behind** the mirror — parallel rays only *appear* to originate there, and C is behind as well.

**Example**
Parallel sunlight on a concave mirror of f = 10 cm concentrates to a bright spot at F, 10 cm in front — the "burning point." The same rays on a convex mirror scatter; tracing them back gives an apparent origin behind the surface.

**Key Point**
Concave = converging, f > 0, F & C in front. Convex = diverging, f < 0, F & C behind.

### 5. Ray Diagrams for Mirrors

**Core Idea**
Three standard rays, each with a known fate, locate any spherical-mirror image.

**Explanation**
1. **Ray 1:** drawn **parallel to the principal axis** → reflects back **through the focal point F** (or, for convex, along a line appearing to come from F).
2. **Ray 2:** drawn **through the focal point** → reflects **parallel to the principal axis**.
3. **Ray 3:** drawn **through the center of curvature C** → strikes the surface along the normal and **reflects back on itself**.

Where two (or three) reflected rays intersect, the image sits; if they diverge, their backward extensions meet at the virtual image.

**Example**
An object beyond C of a concave mirror: Ray 1 leaves the tip parallel and returns through F; Ray 3 passes through C and comes straight back — they cross below the axis between F and C: a real, inverted, reduced image.

**Key Point**
Learn the three fates: ∥ → F; through F → ∥; through C → back on itself. Ray 3 reflects on itself *because* it travels along a radius, i.e., along the normal.

### 6. The Image-Character Table

**Core Idea**
For a concave mirror, the image character depends on where the object stands relative to 2f and f; for a convex mirror, the answer is always the same.

**Explanation**

**Concave mirror (f > 0):**

| Object distance | Image |
|---|---|
| p > 2f | Real, inverted, **reduced** |
| p = 2f | Real, inverted, **same size** (q = p = 2f) |
| 2f > p > f | Real, inverted, **enlarged** |
| p = f | **Image at infinity** (reflected rays parallel) |
| p < f | **Virtual, upright, enlarged** |

**Convex mirror (f < 0):** for an object **anywhere** in front of the mirror, the image is **virtual, upright, and reduced**.

The dramatic feature of the concave table is the **flip at p = f**: outside f, images are real and inverted; exactly at f, they escape to infinity; inside f, they reappear behind the mirror — virtual, upright, enlarged.

**Example**
f = +10 cm: object at 25 cm (p > 2f) → real, inverted, reduced; at 5 cm (p < f) → virtual, upright, doubled. (Worked Example 1.)

**Key Point**
Concave has five zones; convex has one answer. Crossing F flips real/inverted ↔ virtual/upright.

### 7. The Mirror Equation, Power, and Magnification

**Core Idea**
One equation locates the image; one ratio sizes it; one reciprocal powers it.

**Explanation**
The **mirror equation**:

**1/p + 1/q = 2/R** — and since R = 2f: **1/p + 1/q = 1/f**

The **mirror power** (the source's notation: F, in diopters Δ or m⁻¹):

**F = 1/f(m) = 2/R(m)** — with f and R in **meters**.

*Instructor note:* the source uses **F** for both the focal point and the power — a notational collision; many texts use P for power. The meaning is fixed by context and units (diopters).

The **magnification**:

**M = −q/p** (equivalently M = h′/h)

Reading |M|: |M| = 1 → same size; |M| > 1 → enlarged; |M| < 1 → diminished.

**Example**
Concave, f = +10 cm, p = 25 cm: 1/q = 1/10 − 1/25 = 3/50 → q = +16.7 cm; M = −16.7/25 = −0.67 → real, inverted, reduced. (Worked Example 1a.)

**Key Point**
Solve the equation, then *translate the signs*: q's sign → real/virtual; M's sign → upright/inverted; |M| → size.

### 8. Sign Conventions and Reading the Answer

**Core Idea**
Each sign states a physical fact — read them as sentences, not rules to memorize blindly.

**Explanation**

| Quantity | Positive | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Object distance p | Real object (in front of mirror) | Virtual object (behind mirror) |
| Image distance q | Real image (in front of mirror) | Virtual image (behind mirror) |
| Image height h′ | Upright image | Inverted image |
| Magnification M | Upright image | Inverted image |
| Focal length f, radius R | **Concave** mirror | **Convex** mirror |

For real objects (the normal case), p is positive. The mirror type fixes f's sign *before* you compute; q's sign comes *out* of the computation and tells you what formed; M then tells orientation and size.

**Example**
Convex mirror, f = −10 cm, p = 15 cm: 1/q = −1/10 − 1/15 = −1/6 → q = −6 cm (virtual, behind); M = +6/15 = +0.4 (upright, reduced) — exactly the convex-mirror table's prediction. (Worked Example 2.)

**Key Point**
Sign in (f) = mirror type; sign out (q, M) = image character. Every sign is a diagnosis.

## Key Takeaways
1. Vocabulary: p, q, h, h′ (+ upright / − inverted), M = h′/h; F, f, C, R with **R = 2f**; distant objects send parallel rays.
2. Real image: reflected rays intersect, screen-catchable, in front of the mirror, q > 0. Virtual: extensions intersect, not screen-catchable, behind, q < 0.
3. Plane mirror: q = −p, M = +1, virtual, upright — plus the apparent left–right reversal.
4. Concave converges (f > 0, F & C in front); convex diverges (f < 0, F & C behind).
5. Three principal rays: parallel → F; through F → parallel; through C → back on itself.
6. Concave zones: p > 2f (R/I/reduced); p = 2f (R/I/same); 2f > p > f (R/I/enlarged); p = f (infinity); p < f (V/U/enlarged). Convex: always V/U/reduced.
7. 1/p + 1/q = 2/R = 1/f; M = −q/p; power F = 1/f = 2/R in diopters with f in meters.
8. Signs are physics: q+ = real in front; q− = virtual behind; M+ = upright; f+ = concave, f− = convex; |M| vs. 1 gives size.

# 6. WORKED EXAMPLES

**WE 1 — Concave mirror, three object positions (Source Example 1)**
A concave spherical mirror has f = +10.0 cm. Locate and describe the image for (a) p = 25 cm, (b) p = 10.0 cm, (c) p = 5.00 cm.
(a) p = 25 cm:
1. 1/q = 1/f − 1/p = 1/10 − 1/25 = (5 − 2)/50 = 3/50.
2. **q = 50/3 = +16.7 cm** (+ve → real, in front).
3. M = −q/p = −(16.7)/25 = **−0.67** (−ve → inverted; |M| < 1 → diminished).
4. **Real, inverted, diminished** — matches the p > 2f zone (2f = 20 cm).
(b) p = 10.0 cm (= f):
1. 1/q = 1/10 − 1/10 = 0 → **q = ∞: the image is at infinity** (reflected rays emerge parallel).
(c) p = 5.00 cm:
1. 1/q = 1/10 − 1/5 = −1/10 → **q = −10 cm** (−ve → virtual, behind the mirror).
2. M = −(−10)/5 = **+2** (+ve → upright; |M| > 1 → enlarged).
3. **Virtual, upright, doubled** — the p < f (magnifying-mirror) zone.

**WE 2 — Convex mirror (pedagogical example — the source's example set covers only concave)**
A convex mirror has f = −10 cm; an object stands at p = 15 cm. Locate and describe the image.
1. 1/q = 1/f − 1/p = −1/10 − 1/15 = (−3 − 2)/30 = −5/30 = −1/6.
2. **q = −6 cm** → virtual, 6 cm behind the mirror.
3. M = −(−6)/15 = **+0.4** → upright, reduced.
4. Consistent with the convex table: virtual, upright, reduced for *any* p ✓.

**WE 3 — Object at the center of curvature (pedagogical example)**
Concave mirror, f = +12 cm, object at p = 24 cm (which is 2f = C). Find the image.
1. 1/q = 1/12 − 1/24 = 1/24 → **q = +24 cm** — the image forms at C, symmetric with the object.
2. M = −24/24 = **−1** → real, inverted, **same size**.
3. This is the p = 2f zone: q = p = 2f, exactly as the table states.

**WE 4 — Mirror power in diopters (pedagogical example)**
Find the power of (a) a concave mirror with f = +20 cm; (b) a convex mirror with f = −25 cm.
1. Convert to meters: f = +0.20 m and f = −0.25 m.
2. (a) **F = 1/0.20 = +5 D** (concave, converging).
3. (b) **F = 1/(−0.25) = −4 D** (convex, diverging).
Note: the power's sign simply restates the mirror type — and the meters-only rule is strict (f in cm would give answers off by a factor of 100).

**WE 5 — The plane mirror as the R → ∞ limit (derived from the source's own mirror equation)**
Show that a plane mirror's q = −p and M = +1 follow from 1/p + 1/q = 2/R.
1. A plane mirror is a spherical mirror of infinite radius: R → ∞ ⇒ 2/R → 0.
2. 1/p + 1/q = 0 → 1/q = −1/p → **q = −p**.
3. M = −q/p = −(−p)/p = **+1**.
4. With p = 2.0 m: q = −2.0 m — virtual, upright, unmagnified, exactly the two-ray/triangle result.
The general equation contains the plane mirror as its limiting case — a good consistency check on the whole framework.

# 7. COMMON MISTAKES

| # | What students usually do | Why it's wrong | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Drop the minus in M = −q/p | The sign carries the upright/inverted information — losing it throws away half the answer | Compute M fully, then translate: sign → orientation, magnitude → size |
| 2 | Use f > 0 for a convex mirror | f's sign encodes the mirror type: concave +, convex − | Attach the sign to the shape before touching the equation: "converging = positive" |
| 3 | Interpret q < 0 as "no image" | A negative q is a perfectly good **virtual** image — visible, just not projectable | Translate signs instantly: q− → virtual, behind the mirror |
| 4 | Expect to catch a virtual image on a screen | Virtual images are where rays only *appear* to originate — no light lands there | Apply the screen test mentally: real = catchable, virtual = not |
| 5 | Think virtual images can't be seen | "Virtual" sounds like "imaginary" — but every plane-mirror image is virtual and perfectly visible | Say "not *projectable*" instead of "not visible" |
| 6 | Enter p as negative for a real object | p is positive for real objects (in front of the mirror); only virtual objects take −p | Default: real object ⇒ p > 0 |
| 7 | Plug f in centimeters into the power formula | F = 1/f needs **meters** — cm gives a 100× error | Convert to meters before computing diopters |
| 8 | Confuse f and R (use R where f belongs) | R = 2f — mixing them doubles or halves every answer | Label both on every sketch; convert immediately |
| 9 | Draw Ray 3 (through C) as bending at the mirror | A ray through C travels along a radius = the normal, so it reflects **back on itself** | Remember: C-rays retrace their own path |
| 10 | Read M = −2 as "half size" | The minus says inverted; the *2* says doubled | Read M in two steps: sign, then magnitude |

# 8. LECTURE SUMMARY

# Lecture Summary — Image Formation: Mirrors

## What You Need to Know
- The full vocabulary: p, q, h, h′, M, F, f, C, R — and R = 2f.
- The real/virtual distinction in all four aspects.
- Plane-mirror construction and results (q = −p, M = +1).
- Concave vs. convex geometry (which converges; where F and C sit).
- The three principal rays and both image-character tables.
- The mirror equation with sign conventions, magnification, and power in diopters.

## Key Definitions
- **Principal axis** → the normal line to the surface's midpoint (vertex V / pole P).
- **Focal point (F)** → where parallel rays reflect to pass through (or appear to originate from); **focal length (f)** → F-to-mirror distance.
- **Center of curvature (C) / radius (R)** → center/radius of the sphere the surface was cut from; **R = 2f**.
- **Magnification (M)** → M = h′/h = −q/p.
- **Real image** → intersection of actual reflected rays; screen-catchable; in front of the mirror; q > 0.
- **Virtual image** → intersection of ray *extensions*; not screen-catchable; behind the mirror; q < 0.
- **Diopter (Δ)** → unit of mirror power: F = 1/f with f in meters.

## Key Formulas
- R = 2f → geometry of spherical mirrors.
- 1/p + 1/q = 2/R = 1/f → mirror equation.
- M = −q/p (= h′/h) → magnification.
- F = 1/f = 2/R (diopters, f in m) → mirror power.
- q = −p, M = +1 → plane mirror.

## Important Ideas
- Distant objects send parallel rays — the definition of the focal point.
- The plane-mirror result is *provable* (two rays + identical triangles) and is the R → ∞ limit of the general equation.
- Signs are physics in disguise: each sign states where light actually converges or appears to originate.
- The concave mirror has five image zones with a full character flip at p = f; the convex mirror has one answer for all positions.
- Real images of mirrors form *in front* — the reflected light must land somewhere real.

## Common Mistakes
Dropped minus in M; wrong f sign; "q < 0 = no image"; virtual-on-screen attempts; "virtual = invisible"; negative p for real objects; cm in the power formula; f/R mix-up; Ray 3 drawn bent; |M| misread.

## Exam Focus
1. Solving 1/p + 1/q = 1/f with signs, then *fully* characterizing the image (all four attributes).
2. Zone-table reasoning: predicting character from p vs. 2f and f — including the p = f and p = 2f special cases.
3. Convex-mirror problems (where the answer type is fixed and the computation must confirm it).
4. M = −q/p in all directions (find M, find q given M, find p given M).
5. Power in diopters with correct units and sign.

## 60-Second Review
Vocabulary: p, q, h, h′, M; F, f, C, R = 2f; distant → parallel rays. Real: rays meet, screen ✓, front, q+; virtual: extensions, screen ✗, behind, q−. # 9. DIFFICULT CONCEPTS

### Difficult Concept: Real vs. Virtual Images
**Why students struggle:** "Virtual" sounds like "imaginary/invalid," and the distinction is stated abstractly before any construction makes it visible.
**Simple explanation:** Real: the reflected light actually lands at the image point — you can put a screen there. Virtual: the light *diverges* as if from a point where nothing exists — your eye traces it back, but no screen catches anything.
**Intuitive analogy:** A real image is an actual cup on a table; a virtual image is the "person behind the mirror" — you see them clearly, but there's nobody there.
**Step-by-step:** (1) Trace two reflected rays from the object's tip. (2) Do they intersect in front of the mirror? → real, q > 0. (3) Do they diverge, with backward extensions meeting behind? → virtual, q < 0. (4) Apply the screen test to confirm.
**Mini example:** Plane mirror: reflected rays diverge; extensions meet behind → virtual, q = −p.
**Misconception to avoid:** "Virtual images can't be seen" — they're seen constantly; they just can't be *projected*.
**Difficulty: MEDIUM**

### Difficult Concept: Sign Conventions for the Mirror Equation
**Why students struggle:** One equation, five signed quantities, each with a different physical meaning — and the geometric meaning of q flips between mirrors and lenses (next lecture).
**Simple explanation:** Every sign states a physical fact: q > 0 means rays truly converge in front (real); q < 0 means they only appear to originate behind (virtual); f > 0 means a converging (concave) mirror; M > 0 means upright.
**Intuitive analogy:** A medical chart: each ± is a diagnosis — "q positive" reads "light really lands there."
**Step-by-step:** (1) Identify the mirror: concave → f > 0; convex → f < 0. (2) Real object → p > 0. (3) Solve 1/p + 1/q = 1/f for q. (4) Read q: + → real (in front); − → virtual (behind). (5) M = −q/p: sign → orientation; magnitude → size.
**Mini example:** Concave f = 10, p = 5 → q = −10, M = +2: virtual, upright, doubled.
**Misconception to avoid:** "Negative q means no image" — it means a virtual one.
**Difficulty: HARD** → video provided

### Difficult Concept: The Five Zones of a Concave Mirror (the p = f Transition)
**Why students struggle:** Students expect the image to change *gradually* as the object approaches; instead, at p = f the image escapes to infinity, and just inside f it reappears with **every attribute flipped** (real→virtual, inverted→upright).
**Simple explanation:** Outside f, the reflected rays converge in front (real, inverted); at f they emerge parallel (image at infinity); inside f they diverge (virtual, upright, behind the mirror — enlarged).
**Intuitive analogy:** A phase change: heating water warms it smoothly, but at one exact temperature it abruptly boils. Crossing f is the mirror's boiling point.
**Step-by-step:** (1) Compute 2f and f. (2) Compare p to both: p > 2f / = 2f / between / = f / < f. (3) Read the zone from the table. (4) Verify with the equation and M.
**Mini example:** f = 10: p = 25 → real, inverted, reduced; p = 5 → virtual, upright, doubled. Same mirror, opposite characters.
**Misconception to avoid:** "No image forms when p < f" — there is one: virtual, upright, enlarged. And "the image shrinks smoothly as the object nears f" — it *grows* without bound, then flips.
**Difficulty: HARD** → video provided

### Difficult Concept: The Plane Mirror's Left–Right Reversal
**Why students struggle:** Why does the mirror swap left and right but not up and down? It feels arbitrary.
**Simple explanation:** The source's statement: a flat mirror produces an *apparent* left–right reversal — raise your right hand and the image raises the hand on the opposite side. (A full account of the reversal direction is [SOURCE DOES NOT SPECIFY]; the phenomenon itself is the examinable fact.)
**Intuitive analogy:** Pointing at the mirror: your image points *back at you* — the reversal is along the pointing direction, and the hand-swap is how it looks from your viewpoint.
**Step-by-step:** (1) Stand facing a plane mirror. (2) Raise your right hand. (3) The image raises the hand on the mirror-image side. (4) Note that up stays up — the reversal is only left–right *in appearance*.
**Mini example:** Text held up to a mirror reads mirror-written; a single vertical arrow looks unchanged.
**Misconception to avoid:** "Mirrors flip up and down too" — they don't; up remains up.
**Difficulty: MEDIUM**

### Difficult Concept: R = 2f — Radius vs. Focal Length
**Why students struggle:** Two lengths (R and f) describe the same mirror, and problems hand over either one.
**Simple explanation:** For a spherical mirror, parallel rays reflect through a point exactly halfway to the sphere's center: F is the midpoint of the vertex-to-C segment, so R = 2f.
**Intuitive analogy:** The center of a circle and the midpoint of its radius: C is the circle's center; F is the halfway house on the road from the mirror to C.
**Step-by-step:** (1) Identify which of R, f is given. (2) Convert immediately: f = R/2 (or R = 2f). (3) Place F and C on your sketch before any equation. (4) The zone boundaries are 2f = R (that is, C!) and f.
**Mini example:** R = 20 cm → f = 10 cm; the p = 2f zone boundary (20 cm) is exactly the center of curvature.
**Misconception to avoid:** Substituting R where f belongs (or vice versa) — a guaranteed factor-of-2 error.
**Difficulty: EASY**

## SCRIPT 1 — "Signs That Speak: Conquering the Mirror Equation"

**[0:00–0:30] Hook**
The mirror equation is one line of algebra. One line. And yet it destroys more exam grades than any formula in first-year physics. Not because the algebra is hard — because of the plus and minus signs. Today I'm going to show you that every sign in this equation is secretly a *sentence about physics* — and once you can read those sentences, this equation can't hurt you again.

**[0:30–2:00] Concept introduction**
The toolkit. The mirror equation: one over p, plus one over q, equals one over f — and since R equals 2f, that's also two over R. Magnification: M equals minus q over p. That's it. Now the cast. p is the object distance — and for a real object, p is positive. Full stop. q is the image distance. f is the focal length.

And here's where the signs become sentences. f positive means a *concave* mirror — a converging mirror. f negative means *convex* — diverging. You set that sign *before* you compute: it's your input, telling the equation what mirror you own. Then you solve, and q's sign comes *out* — that's the equation telling *you* what happened. q positive: the reflected rays actually converge at a real point in front of the mirror — a real image. You could put a screen there and catch it. q negative: the rays diverge, and your eye traces them backward to a point *behind* the mirror — a virtual image. Perfectly visible — you look at one every morning — but nothing lands there, so no screen can catch it.

And M? M equals minus q over p. M positive: upright. M negative: inverted. And the *magnitude* of M: one means same size; two means doubled; a half means halved.

**[2:00–4:00] Visual explanation**
Let me show you why, not just what. On the left: rays leave the object, reflect, and converge to a point in front of the mirror. Light *actually arrives* there — that's q positive, real, screen-catchable. On the right: rays leave the object, reflect, and *spread apart*. Extend them backward — dashed lines — and they meet behind the mirror, where no light exists. That's q negative, virtual. Your eye does that backward tracing automatically; that's how you see anything in a flat mirror.

So don't memorize the sign table like vocabulary — read it like a chart at a hospital bedside. q positive: "light really lands here." q negative: "light only appears to come from here." f positive: "this mirror converges." M negative: "the image is upside-down." Every sign is a diagnosis.

**[4:00–6:00] Worked example**
The lecture's own example — a concave mirror, f = plus ten centimeters — three object positions. Case one: object at twenty-five. One over q equals one over ten minus one over twenty-five — three fiftieths — q = plus sixteen point seven. Positive: real, in front. M = minus sixteen point seven over twenty-five — minus zero point six seven. Negative: inverted. Magnitude below one: diminished. Real, inverted, diminished. Case two: object at ten — exactly at the focus. One over q equals one over ten minus one over ten — zero. q is infinite: the reflected rays come out parallel; the image is at infinity. Case three: object at five — inside the focus. One over q equals one over ten minus one over five — minus one over ten — q = *minus* ten. Negative: virtual, ten centimeters *behind* the mirror. M = minus (minus ten) over five — plus two. Upright and doubled. That's the magnifying-mirror regime — dentists and makeup mirrors live exactly there.

**[6:00–7:00] Common mistake**
The five classics. One: dropping the minus in M — you lose the entire orientation answer. Two: positive f for a convex mirror — converging gets plus, diverging gets minus, always. Three: reading q negative as "no image" — it's a virtual image; it's *visible*; it's just not catchable. Four: entering p as negative for a real object — real objects get positive p. Five: reading M = minus two as "half size" — the minus says inverted, the *two* says doubled.

**[7:00–8:00] Quick student challenge**
Predict before you compute — that's the discipline. A convex mirror: f = minus fifteen, object at thirty. What will the image be? … Convex mirrors only ever make virtual, upright, reduced images. Now verify: one over q equals minus one over fifteen minus one over thirty — minus three over thirty — q = minus ten centimeters. Virtual, behind. M = plus one-third. Upright, one-third size. Prediction: confirmed. When your prediction and your computation agree, you *know* you're right.

**[8:00–8:30] Final recap**
Ten seconds of sign language: f positive — concave; f negative — convex. q positive — real, in front, screen-catchable; q negative — virtual, behind. M positive — upright; M negative — inverted; magnitude — size. The equation is one line. The signs are the physics. Predict first, then compute.

## SCRIPT 2 — "The Five Zones of a Concave Mirror"

**[0:00–0:30] Hook**
Bring your face slowly toward a shaving mirror. At arm's length: you're upside-down, and small. Closer: bigger, still upside-down. Closer still — at one magic distance — your reflection blows up to infinity and vanishes. And then it comes back… enormous, and right-side up. One mirror. Five personalities. Today we map all five zones, and find the exact distance where everything flips.

**[0:30–2:00] Concept introduction**
Here's the setup. A concave mirror — a converging mirror, focal length f, positive. Its center of curvature C sits at a distance R, and R equals two f. So along the principal axis, in front of the mirror, we have two landmarks: the focal point F, and, twice as far, the center C. Those two landmarks chop the axis into zones — and each zone has its own personality.

Start from far away. A very distant object sends essentially parallel rays — and parallel rays reflect through the focal point. So an object at infinity images at F: real, inverted, and essentially a point. Now let the object come closer, and watch.

**[2:00–4:00] Visual explanation**
Zone one: object beyond C — beyond two f. The rays converge between F and C: a real, inverted, *reduced* image. Small, upside-down, in front of the mirror — projectable on a screen. Zone two: object exactly at C, at two f. Something special happens: the image forms exactly at C too — real, inverted, and *the same size*. Object and image are mirror twins, symmetric about the center. Zone three: object between C and F — between two f and f. Now the image moves beyond C and *grows*: real, inverted, enlarged. Still upside-down, still in front — but bigger than the object. The closer the object gets to F, the farther and larger the image becomes. Zone four: object exactly at F. The reflected rays emerge *parallel* — they never meet. The image has fled to infinity. This is the vanishing point you saw in the mirror. And zone five — the payoff: object *inside* F, closer than the focal length. Now the reflected rays *diverge*. Trace them backward: they meet behind the mirror. Virtual, upright, and enlarged. Everything flipped — real became virtual, inverted became upright — and this is exactly the magnifying regime of a shaving or makeup mirror.

**[4:00–6:00] Worked example**
The lecture's numbers — f = plus ten centimeters. Landmarks: F at ten, C at twenty. Object at twenty-five: that's beyond C, zone one. One over q = one over ten minus one over twenty-five — q = plus sixteen point seven, between F and C where it belongs. M = minus zero point six seven: real, inverted, diminished. Object at ten — exactly at F, zone four: one over q = zero, q at infinity. Parallel rays out; the image has escaped. Object at five — inside F, zone five: one over q = minus one over ten, q = *minus* ten centimeters — ten centimeters behind the mirror. M = plus two: virtual, upright, doubled. Check the story: the closer we got, the bigger the image grew — until at F it left the universe, and returned flipped.

**[6:00–7:00] Common mistake**
Three traps. One: expecting the change to be gradual — like warming water. It's not: crossing F is a *phase change*, water to steam. At F the image is at infinity; just inside F, it's back, virtual and huge. Two: declaring "no image" when the object is inside the focal length. There absolutely is one — virtual, upright, enlarged, behind the mirror. It's the most useful image the mirror makes! Three: forgetting *where* the zone-five image lives: behind the mirror — you can't project it, you can only look at it.

**[7:00–8:00] Quick student challenge**
Two predictions — no computing. Object exactly at two f: where is the image, what size, what orientation? … At two f as well — the center of curvature — real, inverted, same size. Now the object slides just *inside* F. What do you see when you look into the mirror? … An enormous, upright, virtual image of your own eye. And one more: which zone never produces a real image? … Zone five — inside F; and a convex mirror never does either, for any position.

**[8:00–8:30] Final recap**
Five zones, two landmarks. Beyond C: real, inverted, small. At C: real, inverted, twin. Between C and F: real, inverted, big. At F: infinity. Inside F: virtual, upright, huge. Outside F the mirror converges the world; inside F it becomes a magnifier. The flip at the focal point — that's the whole story of the concave mirror.

**T5 — The plane mirror as a limit:** Using the mirror equation 1/p + 1/q = 2/R, show formally that a plane mirror (R → ∞) gives q = −p and M = +1, and interpret the signs.
→ *Key:* 2/R → 0 ⇒ 1/q = −1/p ⇒ q = −p (negative → virtual, behind); M = −(−p)/p = +1 (upright, same size) — the general equation contains the plane mirror as its R → ∞ limit. [MEDIUM — derivation/limit reasoning]

**T6 — Focal length from an observation:** When an object is placed 25 cm from a certain mirror, a real image forms 75 cm in front of the mirror. Find f and M, identify the mirror, and verify the result against the zone table.
→ *Key:* 1/f = 1/25 + 1/75 = 4/75 → f = +18.75 cm (concave); M = −75/25 = −3 → real, inverted, tripled. Zone check: f = 18.75 < 25 < 2f = 37.5 ✓ — the 2f > p > f (enlarged, real, inverted) zone, consistent. [MEDIUM — observation → parameters]
