### 1. Electric Flux — Counting Field Lines Through a Surface

#### Core Idea
Electric flux measures how many electric field lines pass through a surface — strongest when the surface faces the field head-on, zero when the field slides past it edge-on.

#### Explanation
Picture field lines as rain and the surface as a window. Hold the window facing the rain: maximum drops pass through. Turn it edge-on: nothing passes. The definition in the lecture: **electric flux is the number of field lines penetrating a surface perpendicular to them**, equal to the product of the field magnitude and the perpendicular area:

> **Φ_E = E·A**  (units: N·m²/C)

When the surface is tilted so that its perpendicular (normal) makes an angle θ with the field:

> **Φ_E = E⃗·A⃗ = E·A cos θ**

Two landmark orientations:

- **Maximum flux:** surface perpendicular to the field → θ = 0 → cos 0 = 1 → **Φ_max = EA**
- **Zero flux:** surface parallel to the field → θ = 90° → cos 90° = 0 → **Φ_min = 0**

#### Example
The source's example: a 0.16 m × 0.38 m rectangle in a 580 N/C uniform field. Area = 0.0608 m². Facing the field: Φ = 580 × 0.0608 = 35.264 N·m²/C (the source prints 35.364 — apparent typo; method identical). Tilted 30° to the field: see the angle rule below.

#### Key Point
Flux = E·A·cos θ, where **θ is measured from the NORMAL to the surface** — perpendicular surface maximizes it, parallel surface kills it.

---

### 2. The Angle Rule: Normal, Not Surface

#### Core Idea
The θ in Φ = EA cos θ is the angle between the field and the **normal** to the surface — so a surface making 30° with the field uses θ = 60°, not 30°.

#### Explanation
This is the single most dangerous convention in the chapter. When a problem says "the surface makes angle α with the field," the normal makes (90° − α) with the field, and **θ = 90° − α** goes into the formula.

In the source's example: the surface makes 30° with the field → the normal makes 60° → Φ = EA cos 60° = 35.264 × 0.5 = 17.632 N·m²/C (source prints 17.682, following its earlier slip).

Sanity checks that you've used the right angle:
- Surface **parallel** to field → θ = 90° → cos = 0 → Φ = 0 ✓ (matches the "minimum" rule)
- Surface **perpendicular** to field → θ = 0° → cos = 1 → Φ = EA ✓ (matches the "maximum" rule)

#### Example
If a surface makes 60° with the field, use θ = 30°: Φ = EA cos 30° = 0.866 EA.

#### Key Point
Translate first, compute second: surface-to-field angle α ⇒ normal-to-field angle θ = 90° − α ⇒ Φ = EA cos θ.

---

### 3. Gauss's Law — Only the Inside Counts

#### Core Idea
The net electric flux through any **closed** surface equals the net charge **inside** it divided by ε₀ — regardless of the surface's shape, size, or any charges outside.

#### Explanation
For a closed surface (a "Gaussian surface" — a bag fully enclosing a region of space):

> **Gauss's law: Φ = ∯ E⃗·dA⃗ = ∯ E dA cos θ = q_in / ε₀**

with **q_in** = the net charge inside the surface, **ε₀** = permittivity of free space, and θ the angle between the field and the normal to each surface element. Three consequences, all stated in the source:

1. **Shape independence:** deform the surface into any closed shape — sphere, blob, potato — the net flux doesn't change, because q_in doesn't change.
2. **Sign convention:** outward flux counts positive; inward flux counts negative. A surface enclosing net negative charge has negative (inward) net flux.
3. **Where the charge sits inside doesn't matter** — only the net amount does.

#### Example
A closed surface enclosing +3 μC and −1 μC: q_in = +2 μC → Φ = 2 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ ≈ 2.26 × 10⁵ N·m²/C, outward.

#### Key Point
Closed surface ⇒ Φ = q_in/ε₀. Nothing else enters: not shape, not size, not outside charges, not the charge's position inside.

---

### 4. Charges Outside a Closed Surface — The Zero Contribution

#### Core Idea
A charge outside a closed surface contributes **zero net flux**: every field line that enters leaves somewhere else, so entries and exits cancel exactly.

#### Explanation
For any point charge located outside a closed surface: **any field line entering the surface leaves at another point** — the number entering equals the number leaving. With outward counted + and inward counted −, the totals cancel:

> **Net flux through a closed surface surrounding no charge = 0**

The source's summary cases:
- Surface S enclosing q₁: Φ_S = q₁/ε₀
- Surface S' enclosing q₂ and q₃: Φ_S' = (q₂ + q₃)/ε₀ — and if q₂ = −q₃, then Φ_S' = 0 (equal opposite charges cancel)
- Surface S'' enclosing nothing: Φ_S'' = 0

This is why Gauss's law only ever asks about the inside: the outside is guaranteed to wash out.

#### Example
A +50 μC charge sits near a closed surface that encloses only +2 μC. The flux through the surface is 2 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ — the +50 μC contributes lines in and out in perfectly canceling pairs.

#### Key Point
Outside charges: lines in = lines out ⇒ net zero. Enclosed charges: only their **net sum** counts — and opposite charges can cancel to zero flux.

---

### 5. Electric Potential — Energy per Unit Charge

#### Core Idea
Electric potential V is the work done to bring a **unit positive charge from infinity** to a point — a scalar quantity that carries the sign of the source charge.

#### Explanation
Where the field told you "force per unit charge" (a vector, N/C), the potential tells you "energy per unit charge" (a scalar, **volts = J/C**):

> **V = KQ / r**

Properties straight from the source:

- **Scalar:** no direction, no vector components.
- **Sign rule:** V is **positive** near a positive charge, **negative** near a negative charge (the sign travels with Q).
- **Definition:** work done to bring a unit positive charge from infinity to the point.

For **several charges**, potentials simply add — as signed numbers, never as vectors:

> **V_net = V₁ + V₂ + ⋯ + V_n = K Σ (qᵢ/rᵢ)**

and **potential difference** between two points (work done to transfer a unit positive charge between them):

> **ΔV = V_P2 − V_P1 = Kq/r₂ − Kq/r₁**

#### Example
The source's example: q₁ = −50 μC and q₂ = +50 μC. At point A (0.3 m from q₂, 0.6 m from q₁): V_A = K[(−50 μC)/0.6 + (+50 μC)/0.3] = 7.5 × 10⁵ V. At point B (0.4 m from both): V_B = K[(−50 μC)/0.4 + (+50 μC)/0.4] = **0 V** — equal opposite charges at equal distances cancel exactly. (Full solution below.)

#### Key Point
V = KQ/r is a **scalar** with the charge's sign. Multiple potentials add like signed bank balances, not like arrows.

---

### 6. Potential Energy of Charge Systems

#### Core Idea
A pair of charges stores potential energy U = Kq₁q₂/r; a system of three charges stores the sum over all three pairs.

#### Explanation
For two charges separated by r:

> **U = K q₁q₂ / r**  (Joule)

For three charges, add all pairs (three pairs for three charges):

> **U = U₁₂ + U₁₃ + U₂₃ = K [ q₁q₂/r₁₂ + q₁q₃/r₁₃ + q₂q₃/r₂₃ ]**

The signs of the products matter: opposite-sign pairs give negative U, same-sign pairs positive.

#### Example
U for q₁ = +3 μC and q₂ = −5 μC at r = 0.4 m: U = (9 × 10⁹)(3 × 10⁻⁶)(−5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.4 = −0.3375 J — negative for an attractive pair.

#### Key Point
Potential energy lives in *pairs*: U = Kq₁q₂/r per pair, summed over all distinct pairs for a system.

---

### 7. Uniform Fields: ΔV = −Ed, Work, and Energy Direction

#### Core Idea
In a uniform field, potential drops along the field direction: ΔV = −Ed — and a positive charge moving with the field loses potential energy while the field does positive work.

#### Explanation
Place a positive test charge q₀ at point A in a uniform field. It feels F = q₀E and is displaced to B along the field. The source's bookkeeping:

- **A is at higher potential; B is at lower potential.** Field lines are directed **from higher potential to lower potential**.
- **ΔU = U_B − U_A is negative; ΔV = V_B − V_A is negative (V_B < V_A)** — the charge loses potential energy moving with the field.
- The relation between them:

> **ΔV = −Ed  →  E = −ΔV/d**

(The source's phrasing: the electric field equals the negative gradient of the electric potential.)

- Energy and work:

> **ΔU = q₀ΔV = −q₀Ed**
> **W (by the electric force) = −ΔU = −q₀ΔV = q₀Ed = F·d**  (Joule)

The logic chain to memorize: the field does **positive** work on a positive charge moving along E; therefore the system's potential energy **decreases** (W = −ΔU); therefore ΔV along the field direction is **negative**.

#### Example
The source's Example 2: W_AB = +5 × 10⁻⁵ J done by the field on q₀ = +2 × 10⁻⁶ C. Then ΔU = −W = −5 × 10⁻⁵ J and ΔV = ΔU/q₀ = −25 V. (Full solution below.)

#### Key Point
Along the field: V decreases. ΔV = −Ed. Work by the field = −ΔU = −q₀ΔV. Positive charges "fall" toward lower potential.

---

### 8. Equipotential Lines and Surfaces

#### Core Idea
An equipotential is a line/surface on which every point has the same potential — moving along it costs nothing.

#### Explanation
**Definition:** an equipotential line/surface is one on which all points are at the same potential — the potential difference between any two points on it is **zero**.

Four properties from the source:
1. Electric field lines point from **high to low** potential.
2. Electric field lines are **perpendicular** to equipotential lines.
3. Electric field lines have **no component along** an equipotential line.
4. **No work is done** to move a charge along an equipotential line.

Properties 2–4 are one idea in three forms: if E had any component *along* the equipotential, moving along it would change V and require work — contradicting the definition. So the field must cross equipotentials at right angles.

#### Example
Between charged parallel plates, the equipotentials are planes parallel to the plates; the field crosses them perpendicularly, marching from the high-potential (+) plate to the low-potential (−) plate.

#### Key Point
Equipotential ⟂ field lines; ΔV along an equipotential = 0; work along an equipotential = 0.

### Key Takeaways

- Flux = EA cos θ with **θ measured from the surface normal**; perpendicular surface → Φ = EA; parallel surface → Φ = 0.
- A surface making angle α with the field uses θ = 90° − α (the 30°-surface ⇒ cos 60° trap).
- Gauss's law: net flux through any closed surface = q_in/ε₀ — independent of shape, size, and the charge's position inside.
- Outside charges contribute **zero net flux**: lines in = lines out.
- Outward flux is +; inward flux is −; enclosed opposite charges can cancel (q₂ = −q₃ ⇒ Φ = 0).
- Potential: V = KQ/r — a **scalar** in volts (J/C), positive near +Q, negative near −Q; defined as work per unit positive charge from infinity.
- Multiple potentials add as **signed numbers**: V_net = K Σ qᵢ/rᵢ.
- Potential energy: U = Kq₁q₂/r per pair; systems sum over all pairs.
- Uniform field: ΔV = −Ed; E points from high V to low V; ΔU = q₀ΔV; W by field = −ΔU = q₀Ed.
- For any charge: ΔK = −ΔU — the energy lost as potential returns as kinetic (the TV-tube principle).
- Equipotentials: same V everywhere on them; ⟂ to field lines; zero work to move along them.

## 5. FORMULAS

### F1 — Electric flux (perpendicular surface)
**Φ_E = E·A** (N·m²/C)
- **E:** field magnitude (N/C); **A:** area perpendicular to the field (m²).
- **When used:** surface directly facing a uniform field.
- **Interpretation:** the maximum possible flux, Φ_max = EA.

### F2 — Electric flux (angled surface)
**Φ_E = E⃗·A⃗ = E·A cos θ** (N·m²/C)
- **θ:** angle between **E and the normal to the surface** — not the surface itself.
- **When used:** any tilted surface in a uniform field.
- **Interpretation:** θ = 0 → Φ = EA (max); θ = 90° → Φ = 0 (field parallel to surface).
- **Assumptions:** uniform field over the surface; flat surface (source context).

### F3 — Gauss's law
**Φ = ∯ E⃗·dA⃗ = ∯ E dA cos θ = q_in / ε₀**
- **q_in:** net (algebraic) charge enclosed; **ε₀** = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²); **θ:** angle between E and the normal to each surface element.
- **When used:** any closed surface.
- **Interpretation:** flux counts only enclosed charge; outward +, inward −.
- **Assumptions:** closed surface; ε₀ = free-space permittivity; independent of shape and of all external charges.

### F4 — Electric potential of a point charge
**V = KQ / r** (Volt = J/C)
- **Q:** source charge (signed); **r:** distance from it; **K** = 9 × 10⁹.
- **When used:** potential at a point near one charge.
- **Interpretation:** work per unit positive charge brought from infinity; + for +Q, − for −Q; scalar.

### F5 — Superposition of potential
**V_net = K Σ (qᵢ/rᵢ) = K [q₁/r₁ + q₂/r₂ + ⋯ + q_n/r_n]**
- **When used:** several charges.
- **Interpretation:** plain signed addition — no vectors, no directions, no cosines.

### F6 — Potential difference
**ΔV = V_P2 − V_P1 = Kq/r₂ − Kq/r₁** (Volt)
- **When used:** two points around a point charge.
- **Interpretation:** work per unit positive charge transferred between the points.

### F7 — Potential energy of charge pairs
**U = K q₁q₂ / r** (J) ; **U_sys = K [q₁q₂/r₁₂ + q₁q₃/r₁₃ + q₂q₃/r₂₃]** (three charges)
- **When used:** energy stored in a charge configuration.
- **Interpretation:** per-pair energies summed over all distinct pairs; sign of each product matters.

### F8 — Potential difference in a uniform field
**ΔV = −E·d  →  E = −ΔV/d** (V; V/m)
- **d:** displacement **along the field direction** between the two points.
- **When used:** uniform fields (e.g., between parallel plates).
- **Interpretation:** V drops along E; the field points from high to low potential.

### F9 — Energy change of a charge in a field
**ΔU = q₀ΔV = −q₀Ed** (J)
- **When used:** any charge moving through a potential difference.
- **Interpretation:** sign of q₀ flips the story — negative charges lose U when moving to *higher* V.

### F10 — Work done by the electric force
**W = −ΔU = −q₀ΔV = q₀Ed = F·d** (J)
- **When used:** computing the field's work.
- **Interpretation:** field work is the *negative* of the potential-energy change.

### F11 — Kinetic energy from potential (acceleration through ΔV)
**ΔK + ΔU = 0 → ½mv² − 0 = −ΔU → v² = −2ΔU/m**
- **When used:** particle accelerated from rest through a potential difference.
- **Interpretation:** lost potential energy returns as kinetic energy; starting from rest, K₁ = 0.

### Worked Example 1 — Flux through a tilted rectangle (Source: Ch. 2 Example 1)
*A rectangular surface (0.16 m × 0.38 m) is in a uniform field of 580 N/C.*
*a) Maximum possible flux? b) Flux if the surface makes 30° with the field?*

A = 0.16 × 0.38 = 0.0608 m²

**Step 2 — (a) Maximum (surface perpendicular to the field, θ = 0):**

**Step 3 — (b) Translate the angle (the critical step):**
The surface makes 30° **with the field** → the **normal** makes θ = 90° − 30° = **60°** with the field.

**Step 4 — Apply the formula:**
Φ = EA cos 60° = 35.264 × 0.5 = **17.632 N·m²/C** *(source prints 17.682, following the same slip)*

**Sanity check:** half the maximum — exactly what cos 60° should give. ✓

### Worked Example 2 — Gauss's law with mixed charges (Source: Ch. 2 Problem)
*Ten charges: q₁ = q₃ = q₈ = q₉ = +2 μC and q₂ = q₄ = q₅ = q₆ = q₇ = q₁₀ = −2 μC. Find the flux through surfaces S₁, S₂, S₃, which enclose (per the source's figure): S₁ → {q₁, q₂, q₃, q₄, q₅, q₇, q₁₀}; S₂ → {q₄, q₅, q₆, q₁₀}; S₃ → {q₇, q₈, q₉, q₁₀}.*

**Step 1 — S₁: sum the enclosed charges (algebraic):**
q_in = (2 − 2 + 2 − 2 − 2 − 2 − 2) × 10⁻⁶ = **−6 × 10⁻⁶ C**

**Step 2 — Apply Gauss's law:**
Φ_S1 = −6 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ N·m²/C — **negative ⇒ inward flux** ✓

**Step 3 — S₂:**
q_in = (−2 − 2 − 2 − 2) × 10⁻⁶ = −8 × 10⁻⁶ C
Φ_S2 = **−8 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀** N·m²/C (inward) ✓

**Step 4 — S₃:**
q_in = (−2 + 2 + 2 − 2) × 10⁻⁶ = 0
Φ_S3 = **0** ✓ — the enclosed charges cancel exactly.

### Worked Example 3 — Potential at two points (Source: Ch. 3 Example 1)
*Charges q₁ = −50 μC and q₂ = +50 μC. Point A lies 0.3 m from q₂ and 0.6 m from q₁; point B lies 0.4 m from each charge [distances per the source's figure/solution]. Find V_A and V_B.*

**Step 1 — A: scalar sum with signs:**
V_A = K[q₁/r_A1 + q₂/r_A2] = 9 × 10⁹[(−50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.6 + (50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.3]

**Step 2 — Compute term by term:**
(−50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.6 = −8.33 × 10⁻⁵ ; (50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.3 = +1.67 × 10⁻⁴

**Step 3 — Sum and multiply:**
V_A = 9 × 10⁹ × (8.33 × 10⁻⁵) = **7.5 × 10⁵ V** ✓

**Step 4 — B (equidistant from the equal-and-opposite pair):**
V_B = 9 × 10⁹[(−50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.4 + (50 × 10⁻⁶)/0.4] = 9 × 10⁹ × 0 = **0 V** ✓

**Insight:** equal opposite charges at equal distances → exact cancellation. No vectors were needed anywhere — potentials just add.

### Worked Example 4 — From work to ΔU and ΔV (Source: Ch. 3 Example 2)
*The work done by the electric force as a test charge q₀ = +2 × 10⁻⁶ C moves from A to B is W_AB = +5 × 10⁻⁵ J. Find ΔU = U_B − U_A and ΔV = V_B − V_A.*

**Step 1 — Energy change (work by the field = negative of ΔU):**
ΔU = −W_AB = **−5 × 10⁻⁵ J**

**Step 2 — Potential difference (energy per unit charge):**
ΔV = ΔU/q₀ = (−5 × 10⁻⁵)/(2 × 10⁻⁶) = **−25 V**

**Interpretation:** the charge moved with the field to a lower potential, losing potential energy while the field did positive work.

### Worked Example 5 — ΔU = qΔV for both charge signs (Source: Ch. 3 Example 3)
*ΔV = V_B − V_A = +24 V (B at higher potential). Find ΔU for (a) q = +2.2 μC, (b) q = −1.1 μC.*

**Step 1 — (a) Positive charge:**
ΔU = qΔV = (2.2 × 10⁻⁶)(24) = **+5.28 × 10⁻⁵ J** — the positive charge gained potential energy climbing to B.

**Step 2 — (b) Negative charge:**
ΔU = (−1.1 × 10⁻⁶)(24) = **−2.64 × 10⁻⁵ J** — the negative charge *lost* potential energy moving to the higher potential. Same ΔV, opposite energy story: the sign of q flips everything.

### Worked Example 6 — The TV tube: electron and proton through 5000 V (Source: Ch. 3 Example 4)
*An electron is accelerated from rest through 5000 V. (a) ΔU? (b) final speed? (c) Repeat for a proton through 5000 V. (mₑ = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg, m_p = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg)*

**(a) Step 1 — Electron (moves from low to high potential ⇒ ΔV = +5000 V):**
ΔU = qΔV = (−1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(5000) = **−8 × 10⁻¹⁶ J**
*(Negative ⇒ the electron loses potential energy moving to the higher potential.)*

**(b) Step 2 — Conservation of energy:**
ΔK + ΔU = 0 → ½mv² − 0 = −ΔU

**Step 3 — Solve:**
v² = −2ΔU/mₑ = −2(−8 × 10⁻¹⁶)/(9.1 × 10⁻³¹) = 1.76 × 10¹⁵
**v = 4.19 × 10⁶ m/s** ✓

**(c) Step 4 — Proton (moves from the +ve terminal to the −ve terminal ⇒ ΔV = −5000 V):**
q_p = +1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C
ΔU = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹)(−5000) = **−8 × 10⁻¹⁶ J** (also loses PE — moving from high to low potential)

**Step 5 — Speed:**
v² = −2ΔU/m_p = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁵)/(1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 9.58 × 10¹¹
**v = 9.79 × 10⁵ m/s** ✓

**Key observation:** both particles lost the *same* |ΔU| = 8 × 10⁻¹⁶ J, yet the electron ends up ~4× faster — the mass difference (mₑ ≪ m_p) does that.

## 7. COMMON MISTAKES

**Mistake 1 — Using the surface angle instead of the normal angle.**
- *What students do:* plug the given "30° with the field" straight into cos.
- *Why it's wrong:* θ is defined between E and the **normal**; a 30°-tilted surface needs cos 60°.
- *How to avoid:* always convert first: θ = 90° − α. Check with landmarks: parallel surface ⇒ θ = 90° ⇒ Φ = 0.

**Mistake 2 — Expecting nonzero flux through a field-parallel surface.**
- *What students do:* compute EA for a surface lying in the field's plane.
- *Why it's wrong:* the field slides along the surface, penetrating nothing: cos 90° = 0.
- *How to avoid:* picture the rain/window: edge-on window catches no rain.

**Mistake 3 — Including external charges in Gauss's law.**
- *What students do:* add a nearby outside charge into q_in.
- *Why it's wrong:* outside charges send equal lines in and out — net contribution zero, always.
- *How to avoid:* draw the surface first, then physically circle what's inside; only the circled set enters q_in.

**Mistake 4 — Believing flux depends on surface shape or size.**
- *What students do:* assume a bigger or rounder surface "catches more" for the same enclosed charge.
- *Why it's wrong:* Gauss's law is explicitly shape- and size-independent — q_in alone sets Φ.
- *How to avoid:* repeat the mantra: same enclosed charge ⇒ same net flux, any closed shape.

**Mistake 5 — Dropping the flux sign.**
- *What students do:* report |Φ| and lose the inward/outward information.
- *Why it's wrong:* negative net flux (net inward) is physically distinct: it signals net negative enclosed charge.
- *How to avoid:* carry the sign of q_in through the division and interpret it at the end.

**Mistake 6 — Superposing potentials as vectors.**
- *What students do:* hunt for directions or components when combining V's.
- *Why it's wrong:* potential is a scalar — there is nothing to resolve.
- *How to avoid:* treat each Kq/r as a signed bank balance; just add them.

**Mistake 7 — Discarding the signs of charges in potential sums.**
- *What students do:* use |q| in every term of V = K Σ qᵢ/rᵢ.
- *Why it's wrong:* the sign is the whole point — it's how +50 μC and −50 μC cancel to V_B = 0 at equal distances.
- *How to avoid:* enter charges with their signs in every potential calculation.

**Mistake 8 — Applying positive-charge intuition to electrons in ΔU = qΔV.**
- *What students do:* assume moving to higher potential always costs energy.
- *Why it's wrong:* with q negative, ΔU = qΔV flips: the electron *loses* energy going to higher V (that's how it accelerates in a TV tube).
- *How to avoid:* never decide the energy story before multiplying the two signs.

**Mistake 9 — Sign of the field's work.**
- *What students do:* write W = +ΔU, or confuse "work by the field" with "work against the field."
- *Why it's wrong:* the field's work is the negative of the potential-energy change: W = −ΔU.
- *How to avoid:* fixed chain: field does positive work ⇒ energy drops ⇒ W = −ΔU = −q₀ΔV = q₀Ed.

**Mistake 10 — Thinking work is needed along an equipotential.**
- *What students do:* compute W = qΔV with a nonzero ΔV between points on the same equipotential.
- *Why it's wrong:* by definition ΔV = 0 between such points ⇒ W = 0.
- *How to avoid:* identify equipotentials first; only crossings *between* different equipotentials involve work.

## 8. LECTURE SUMMARY

# Lecture Summary

## What You Need to Know
- Flux: Φ = EA cos θ, θ between E and the **normal**; max = EA (θ = 0), zero (θ = 90°, surface parallel to field).
- Surface at angle α to the field ⇒ use θ = 90° − α.
- Gauss's law: Φ_closed = q_in/ε₀ — shape-, size-, position-independent; outside charges give zero net flux (lines in = lines out).
- Outward flux +, inward −; enclosed opposite charges can cancel to zero.
- Potential: V = KQ/r — scalar, volts (J/C), + near +Q, − near −Q; work per unit positive charge from infinity.
- Potentials superpose as signed numbers: V_net = K Σ qᵢ/rᵢ.
- Potential difference: ΔV = Kq/r₂ − Kq/r₁; pair energy U = Kq₁q₂/r; systems sum over pairs.
- Uniform field: ΔV = −Ed; E points from high V to low V; ΔU = q₀ΔV; W_field = −ΔU = q₀Ed.
- Energy conservation: ΔK = −ΔU ⇒ v = √(−2ΔU/m) from rest.
- Equipotential: same V everywhere; ⟂ to field lines; zero work along it.

## Key Definitions
- **Electric flux (Φ_E)** → number of field lines penetrating a surface; E × perpendicular area; N·m²/C.
- **Gauss's law** → net flux through any closed surface = q_in/ε₀, independent of shape.
- **q_in** → net (algebraic) charge enclosed by the closed surface.
- **Electric potential (V)** → work done bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to a point; scalar; volt.
- **Potential difference (ΔV)** → work per unit positive charge transferred between two points.
- **Potential energy (U)** → Kq₁q₂/r per pair of charges; joule.
- **Equipotential line/surface** → locus of equal potential; ΔV = 0 between its points.

## Key Formulas
- **Φ = EA cos θ** → flux through a tilted surface (θ from the normal).
- **Φ = q_in/ε₀** → Gauss's law for closed surfaces.
- **V = KQ/r** → point-charge potential (scalar, signed).
- **V_net = K Σ qᵢ/rᵢ** → scalar superposition.
- **ΔV = Kq/r₂ − Kq/r₁** → potential difference.
- **U = Kq₁q₂/r; U_sys = Σ pairs** → potential energy.
- **ΔV = −Ed; E = −ΔV/d** → uniform field ↔ potential.
- **ΔU = q₀ΔV; W = −ΔU = q₀Ed** → energy and work.
- **½mv² = −ΔU** → speed after acceleration through ΔV.

## Important Ideas
- Gauss's law turns a *surface integral problem* into a *bookkeeping problem*: count the enclosed charge, divide by ε₀, done.
- Potential is the field's scalar twin: no directions anywhere — signs do all the work.
- The sign of q in ΔU = qΔV is a "master switch": electrons run the positive-charge story backwards.
- Same |ΔU| gives different speeds for different masses (electron 4.19 × 10⁶ vs. proton 9.79 × 10⁵ m/s through 5000 V).
- Zero net flux does NOT mean zero field — it means zero *net enclosed charge*.

## Common Mistakes
- Surface angle vs. normal angle (30° ⇒ cos 60°).
- External charges in q_in; shape/size assumptions.
- Vector-adding potentials; dropping charge signs.
- Positive-charge intuition applied to electrons.
- W = +ΔU sign error; work imagined along equipotentials.

## Exam Focus
The concepts most likely to demand real understanding:
1. **The angle translation** in flux problems (the single most common lost mark).
2. **Gauss's law bookkeeping** with mixed-sign enclosed sets and the inward/outward interpretation.
3. **ΔU = qΔV sign logic**, especially negative charges and W = −ΔU.
4. **Speed-after-acceleration** problems (TV-tube pattern) with the electron/proton contrast.

## 60-Second Review
Flux counts field lines through a surface: Φ = EA cos θ — θ from the NORMAL, so a 30°-tilted surface uses cos 60°; parallel surface catches nothing. Closed surface? Gauss: Φ = q_in/ε₀. Outside charges wash out (lines in = lines out); shape is irrelevant; negative q_in means inward flux. Potential is the scalar cousin: V = KQ/r, signed, added like bank balances; equal opposite charges at equal distances cancel. Energy: U = Kq₁q₂/r per pair. Uniform field: V drops along E (ΔV = −Ed); ΔU = qΔV — negative q flips the story; field work = −ΔU. Accelerated from rest: ½mv² = |ΔU|. Equipotentials: ⟂ to field, zero work along them.

## 9. DIFFICULT CONCEPTS

### Difficult Concept: The Normal-Angle Convention in Flux

**Why students struggle:** The problem hands them one angle (surface-to-field) while the formula wants a different one (normal-to-field). Under time pressure they plug the given angle directly.
**Simple explanation:** The formula's θ measures how far the surface's *perpendicular stick* is tilted from the field. If the surface is tilted 30° from the field, its stick is tilted 60°.
**Intuitive analogy:** Sunlight through a window: the glass is tilted 30° from the sun's rays means the window's "facing direction" is tilted 60° away from the sun — and it's the facing direction that determines how much sunlight gets through.
**Step-by-step explanation:** (1) Read the given angle α (surface to field). (2) Compute θ = 90° − α. (3) Substitute into Φ = EA cos θ. (4) Verify with landmarks: α = 0 (surface ⟂ field) ⇒ θ = 0 ⇒ Φ = EA ✓; α = 90° (surface ∥ field) ⇒ θ = 90° ⇒ Φ = 0 ✓.
**Mini example:** Surface at 60° to the field ⇒ θ = 30° ⇒ Φ = 0.866 EA.
**Misconception to avoid:** "θ is the angle the problem gives me." Only after translation — always.
**Difficulty:** MEDIUM

### Difficult Concept: Gauss's Law — Only Inside Counts

**Why students struggle:** Intuition says nearby charges and bigger surfaces should "catch more lines." Accepting that net flux depends *only* on enclosed net charge — nothing else — requires overriding strong geometric instincts.
**Simple explanation:** A closed surface is a net stretched around a region. Lines from outside charges poke through the net going in and come back out — nothing is *caught*. Lines from inside charges are stuck crossing the boundary once, outward (or inward for negative). Count what's caught: that's q_in/ε₀.
**Intuitive analogy:** A fishing net in a river: fish that swim through and out the other side change nothing. Only the fish *trapped inside* the net when you pull it up are counted — and it doesn't matter whether the net is round, box-shaped, or crumpled.
**Step-by-step explanation:** (1) Draw the closed surface. (2) Circle every charge physically inside it. (3) Sum those charges algebraically (signs matter) → q_in. (4) Divide: Φ = q_in/ε₀. (5) Interpret: Φ > 0 outward, Φ < 0 inward, Φ = 0 no net enclosed charge. (6) Ignore every outside charge — guaranteed zero contribution.
**Mini example:** +2 μC inside, −5 μC outside → Φ = 2 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ (the −5 μC contributes nothing).
**Misconception to avoid:** "Zero flux means zero field on the surface." No — it means zero *net enclosed charge*; the field can be strong everywhere.
**Difficulty:** HARD

### Difficult Concept: Scalar Superposition of Potential

**Why students struggle:** Weeks of vector training (forces, fields) create a reflex: "opposite directions cancel." Potential has no directions, so that reflex misfires — yet students keep looking for geometry to do.
**Simple explanation:** Each charge writes a signed number, Kq/r, at the point. The total potential is the arithmetic sum of those numbers. Distance enters; direction never does.
**Intuitive analogy:** Bank accounts: a deposit (+) and an equal withdrawal (−) balance to zero regardless of *which bank teller windows* they passed through. Position doesn't matter; only the signed amounts do.
**Step-by-step explanation:** (1) List every charge with its sign. (2) List each charge's distance to the point. (3) Compute each Vᵢ = Kqᵢ/rᵢ *with sign*. (4) Add the numbers. (5) Done — no components, no cosines.
**Mini example:** +50 μC at 0.4 m and −50 μC at 0.4 m from B: V = K(50 μC/0.4) + K(−50 μC/0.4) = 0.
**Misconception to avoid:** "Equal opposite charges always cancel." Only at equal distances — otherwise the closer one wins (point A in the worked example: 7.5 × 10⁵ V).
**Difficulty:** MEDIUM

### Difficult Concept: Energy Bookkeeping — ΔU = qΔV, W = −ΔU, and the Electron

**Why students struggle:** Three sign-bearing quantities (q, ΔV, W) interact, and the electron behaves opposite to every positive-charge instinct: it *gains* kinetic energy moving to *higher* potential.
**Simple explanation:** One multiplication decides everything: ΔU = qΔV. For a positive charge, climbing to higher V costs energy; for a negative charge, the same trip *releases* energy. And whatever potential energy the charge loses, the field gains as work done (W = −ΔU) — and the particle gains as kinetic energy (ΔK = −ΔU).
**Intuitive analogy:** A ledger: ΔU is the change in your savings balance, W is the cash the field hands you — every pound the balance drops is a pound in your hand. The sign of q decides whether "moving up the potential hill" is a withdrawal or a deposit.
**Step-by-step explanation:** (1) Fix the direction of travel and write ΔV = V_final − V_initial with its sign. (2) Write q with its sign. (3) ΔU = qΔV — multiply the signs. (4) Field's work: W = −ΔU. (5) If released from rest: ΔK = −ΔU, so ½mv² = |ΔU|. (6) Interpret the energy story in words before boxing numbers.
**Mini example:** Electron through ΔV = +100 V: ΔU = (−e)(+100) = −1.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ J → it *loses* potential energy and emerges faster — the opposite of a positive charge's experience.
**Misconception to avoid:** "Higher potential always means higher energy." True only for positive charges; electrons run the story backwards.
**Difficulty:** HARD

### Difficult Concept: Equipotential Geometry

**Why students struggle:** Connecting "perpendicular to field lines" to "zero work" is an abstract chain: no E-component along the surface ⇒ no force component along the motion ⇒ no work.
**Simple explanation:** An equipotential is level ground for electricity. The field always points across it, never along it — so walking along an equipotential means pushing nothing uphill.
**Intuitive analogy:** Contour lines on a hiking map: walking exactly along a contour line means zero climbing. The slope (field) points straight across the contour lines, never along them.
**Step-by-step explanation:** (1) Definition: all points on the line have equal V. (2) So ΔV = 0 between any two points on it. (3) So W = qΔV = 0 for motion along it. (4) So the field's force has no component along the line. (5) Therefore E must be perpendicular to the equipotential. (6) And field lines run from high-V to low-V equipotentials.
**Mini example:** Between parallel plates, equipotentials are flat sheets parallel to the plates; crossing from one sheet to the next costs qΔV; sliding along a sheet costs nothing.
**Misconception to avoid:** "A charge can't move on an equipotential because the field is there." It can — the field just does no work on that motion.
**Difficulty:** MEDIUM

Now, the question that matters: when a charge q moves across that height difference, how does its potential energy change? One formula answers everything: **delta-U equals q times delta-V**. And the deep thing about that formula is that it's a *multiplication of signs*. For a positive charge, climbing to a higher potential — positive delta-V — costs energy: positive delta-U. Marbles go downhill. Fine. But now flip the charge. An electron has q *negative*. Negative times positive delta-V is *negative* delta-U. The electron, moving to the higher potential, *loses* potential energy. It's a marble that rolls *uphill* — gaining speed the whole way. The rule didn't break; the sign flipped the story. And where does that lost energy go? Straight into kinetic energy — that's conservation: delta-K plus delta-U equals zero. Every joule the ledger loses becomes motion."

**[2:00–4:00] Visual explanation**
"Let's set up the ledger properly, because the signs are where everyone slips. Two parallel plates. The field runs from the positive plate to the negative plate — from high potential to low. On the left of your notebook, track the charge's potential energy. On the right, track its kinetic energy. They always trade — the total never changes. Now, two travelers. A positive test charge, released near the positive plate: it moves *with* the field, toward lower V. Delta-V is negative, q is positive — delta-U negative. Left column drops, right column rises, the marble rolls downhill. Now the electron, released near the *negative* plate: the field pushes it *against* the field direction — toward the positive plate, toward *higher* V. Delta-V positive, q negative — delta-U negative *again*. Left column drops, right column rises — the electron accelerates 'uphill.' Different direction, same ledger: energy leaves the potential account and shows up as speed. One more entry for completeness: the *work* done by the electric force. That's just the *withdrawal* from the potential account: W equals minus delta-U — equals minus q delta-V. The field hands the particle exactly the joules its potential energy gave up. Memorize the chain: work by the field is minus delta-U; and from rest, one-half m-v-squared equals the magnitude of delta-U. Speed, work, energy — all one ledger."

**[4:00–6:00] Worked example**
"The lecture's TV-tube example, numbers and all. An electron, starting from rest, is accelerated through five thousand volts. Question one: the energy change. Delta-U equals q delta-V — minus one point six times ten to the minus nineteen, times five thousand — *minus* eight times ten to the minus sixteen joules. Negative: the electron lost potential energy moving to the higher potential. Question two: the speed. From rest, one-half m v-squared equals the magnitude of delta-U: v-squared equals two times eight times ten to the minus sixteen, divided by nine point one times ten to the minus thirty-one. That's one point seven six times ten to the fifteenth — v equals four point one nine times ten to the sixth, about four point two million meters per second. Now the twist: send a *proton* through the same tube. The proton moves from the positive terminal to the negative — so its delta-V is *minus* five thousand. But its charge is *plus* e. Minus times plus-five-thousand-times... q delta-V: positive one point six times ten to the minus nineteen, times minus five thousand — again minus eight times ten to the minus sixteen joules. Same energy payout! And the speed? Same one-half m v-squared, but with the proton's mass — one point six seven times ten to the minus twenty-seven, nearly two thousand times heavier. v equals nine point seven nine times ten to the fifth — about a million meters per second. Same energy, quarter the speed. Mass is the only difference. That's accelerators in a nutshell."

**[6:00–7:00] Common mistake**
"Three slips, all about signs. Slip one — the big one: applying marble intuition to electrons. 'Moving to higher potential costs energy.' For a positive charge, yes. For an electron, never — the negative q flips the sign of delta-U. Before you judge the energy story, multiply the two signs; don't *feel* it, *compute* it. Slip two: writing work as plus delta-U. The field's work is the *withdrawal* from the potential account: W equals minus delta-U, always. If your work and your energy change come out with the same sign, you've double-counted. Slip three: assuming the same voltage gives the same speed to every particle. Same voltage gives the same *energy* — speed depends on mass. Electron and proton through five thousand volts: identical energy payout, speeds differing by almost a factor of four."

**[7:00–8:00] Quick student challenge**
"Your turn, two numbers, then a judgment. An electron accelerates from rest through one hundred volts. Find delta-U. Then find its final speed. Pause — do both. … Delta-U: q delta-V — one point six times ten to the minus nineteen, times one hundred — minus one point six times ten to the minus seventeen joules. Negative, as it must be for our backwards climber. Speed: one-half m v-squared equals one point six times ten to the minus seventeen — v-squared equals three point two times ten to the minus seventeen over nine point one times ten to the minus thirty-one — three point five times ten to the thirteenth. Square root: about five point nine times ten to the six meters per second. From just a hundred volts — a AA-battery stack's worth of potential. That's why electrons were the workhorses of every tube-era device: tiny mass, huge payoff per volt."

**[8:00–8:30] Final recap**
"The ledger in thirty seconds. Potential difference is an electrical height. Delta-U equals q times delta-V — and the sign of q is the master switch: positive charges pay to climb; electrons *get paid* to climb. The field's work is minus delta-U — the withdrawal from the energy account. Released from rest, every joule lost becomes speed: one-half m v squared equals the magnitude of delta-U — same voltage, same energy, lighter particle, faster exit. Sign first, magnitude second, mass last. The electron isn't breaking the rules — it's just reading the ledger from the other side."

## 12. PRACTICE QUESTIONS

*(Student-facing questions. Instructor keys are for platform use and must not be displayed with the question.)*

### LEVEL 1 — UNDERSTAND

**Q1.** Define electric flux and state its SI unit. When is flux through a surface zero?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* The number of field lines penetrating a surface = EA cos θ; unit N·m²/C; zero when the surface is parallel to the field (θ = 90°). *Skill:* flux definition. *Difficulty:* EASY.

**Q2.** State Gauss's law in words. Does the net flux depend on the shape of the closed surface or on charges outside it?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* Net flux through any closed surface = net enclosed charge ÷ ε₀; independent of shape and of all external charges (lines in = lines out). *Skill:* Gauss statement. *Difficulty:* EASY.

**Q3.** Define electric potential. Is it a vector or a scalar, and what is its sign near a negative charge?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* Work done to bring a unit positive charge from infinity to the point; scalar; negative near a negative charge. *Skill:* potential definition. *Difficulty:* EASY.

**Q4.** In a uniform field pointing from A to B, which point is at higher potential? What is the relation ΔV = V_B − V_A in terms of E and d?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* A (field lines run high → low V); ΔV = −Ed. *Skill:* field–potential direction rule. *Difficulty:* EASY.

**Q5.** State the four properties of equipotential lines.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* (1) field lines point high → low potential; (2) field lines ⟂ equipotentials; (3) no field component along an equipotential; (4) no work done moving along one. *Skill:* equipotential properties. *Difficulty:* EASY.

**Q6.** Why does an outside charge contribute zero net flux through a closed surface?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* Every field line entering leaves elsewhere — inward (−) and outward (+) crossings cancel exactly. *Skill:* external-charge logic. *Difficulty:* EASY–MEDIUM.

### LEVEL 2 — APPLY

**Q7.** A flat surface of area 0.5 m² sits in a uniform field of 400 N/C. Find the flux when (a) the surface is perpendicular to the field; (b) the surface makes 60° with the field; (c) the surface is parallel to the field.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* (a) Φ = EA = 200 N·m²/C; (b) surface-to-field 60° ⇒ θ = 30° ⇒ Φ = 200 cos 30° ≈ 173.2 N·m²/C; (c) Φ = 0. *Skill:* angle translation + flux computation. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q8.** A closed surface encloses charges +3 μC and −1 μC. Compute the net flux (magnitude and direction character), with ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²).
> *Instructor key — Answer:* q_in = +2 μC; Φ = 2 × 10⁻⁶/8.85 × 10⁻¹² ≈ 2.26 × 10⁵ N·m²/C, outward (positive). *Skill:* Gauss computation. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q9.** Find the electric potential at a point 0.2 m from a +5 μC charge.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* V = KQ/r = (9 × 10⁹)(5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.2 = 2.25 × 10⁵ V. *Skill:* point potential. *Difficulty:* EASY–MEDIUM.

**Q10.** Charge q₁ = +6 μC is 0.3 m from point P; charge q₂ = −6 μC is 0.9 m from P. Find V at P.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* V = 9 × 10⁹[(6 × 10⁻⁶)/0.3 + (−6 × 10⁻⁶)/0.9] = 9 × 10⁹ × 6 × 10⁻⁶(3.333 − 1.111) = 1.2 × 10⁵ V. *Skill:* signed scalar superposition. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q11.** A charge of +4 μC crosses a potential difference ΔV = +12 V; then a charge of −2 μC makes the same trip. Find ΔU for each.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* ΔU = qΔV: (+4 × 10⁻⁶)(12) = +4.8 × 10⁻⁵ J; (−2 × 10⁻⁶)(12) = −2.4 × 10⁻⁵ J. *Skill:* sign multiplication. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q12.** Compute the potential energy of a pair: q₁ = +3 μC and q₂ = −5 μC separated by 0.4 m.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* U = Kq₁q₂/r = (9 × 10⁹)(3 × 10⁻⁶)(−5 × 10⁻⁶)/0.4 = −0.3375 J (negative — attractive pair). *Skill:* pair energy. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

## 13. TRANSFER QUESTIONS

### LEVEL 3 — TRANSFER

**Q13.** A square surface of side 0.5 m is placed in a uniform field of 600 N/C so that the surface makes 40° with the field. Compute the flux through it.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* A = 0.25 m²; surface-to-field 40° ⇒ θ = 50°; Φ = EA cos 50° = 600 × 0.25 × 0.643 ≈ 96.4 N·m²/C. *Skill:* angle translation in a new setting. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q14.** A closed surface S encloses +5 μC. Two external charges, +10 μC and −10 μC, are brought close to S. (a) What is the flux through S? (b) The +5 μC is moved outside S and a −3 μC is placed inside. Now what is the flux, and what does its sign mean?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* (a) Φ = 5 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ ≈ 5.65 × 10⁵ N·m²/C — the external pair changes nothing (and their sum is zero anyway, each contributing zero individually). (b) q_in = −3 μC → Φ = −3 × 10⁻⁶/ε₀ ≈ −3.39 × 10⁵ N·m²/C — negative means net *inward* flux. *Skill:* Gauss with changing enclosures + sign interpretation. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM–HARD.

**Q15.** The same charge +q is enclosed successively by a sphere, then an irregular potato-shaped surface, then a crumpled bag. Compare the net flux through the three surfaces and justify using Gauss's law.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* Identical in all three: Φ = q/ε₀. Gauss's law depends only on enclosed charge — shape and size are irrelevant. *Skill:* shape-independence reasoning. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.

**Q16.** Two charges, +q and −q (equal magnitudes), are fixed in space. Describe the set of all points where the total potential is zero, and justify using scalar superposition.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* V = Kq(1/r₊ − 1/r₋) = 0 ⇒ r₊ = r₋ — every point equidistant from both charges: the plane that perpendicularly bisects the line joining them. (Contrast: the *field* is not zero there — potential and field are different quantities.) *Skill:* scalar superposition transferred to a locus argument. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM–HARD.

**Q17.** Three charges: q₁ = +2 μC, q₂ = +2 μC, q₃ = −3 μC, with r₁₂ = 0.3 m, r₁₃ = 0.4 m, r₂₃ = 0.5 m. Compute the total potential energy of the system.
> *Instructor key — Answer:* U = K[q₁q₂/r₁₂ + q₁q₃/r₁₃ + q₂q₃/r₂₃] = 9 × 10⁹[(4 × 10⁻¹²)/0.3 + (−6 × 10⁻¹²)/0.4 + (−6 × 10⁻¹²)/0.5] = 9 × 10⁹ × (13.33 − 15 − 12) × 10⁻¹² = **−0.123 J**. *Skill:* multi-pair system energy. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM–HARD.

**Q18.** Through what potential difference must an electron, starting from rest, be accelerated to reach a speed of 1.9 × 10⁶ m/s? (mₑ = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg)
> *Instructor key — Answer:* Required KE = ½mₑv² = ½(9.1 × 10⁻³¹)(1.9 × 10⁶)² ≈ 1.64 × 10⁻¹⁸ J = |ΔU| = e|ΔV| ⇒ |ΔV| = 1.64 × 10⁻¹⁸/1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 10.3 V. *Skill:* inverting the acceleration formula — transfer of the TV-tube pattern. *Difficulty:* HARD.

**Q19.** A proton drifts along an equipotential line from point X to point Y inside a uniform field. What is the work done by the field, and what can you say about the field's direction relative to the line XY?
> *Instructor key — Answer:* W = 0 (ΔV = 0 between points on an equipotential ⇒ W = qΔV = 0); the field must be perpendicular to the equipotential line — it has no component along XY. *Skill:* equipotential properties applied. *Difficulty:* MEDIUM.
